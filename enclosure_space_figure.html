Mink fur farming: enclosure space and the cost of raising the standard

A schematic of what a single mink is allowed under each fur-farming standard (green = floor area, blue = open-water pool), with the one-time and per-pelt cost of moving the Finnish industry to that standard. Every reform standard that has been costed is well above the latest annual pelt price of about EUR 29 (the 2024 average, in 2025 prices): the current cages already run at a loss.

Fur farming already banned or being phased out across the EU

Full national ban in force: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria (mink), Croatia, Czechia, France, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Slovakia, Slovenia.

Phase-out under way: Estonia (2026), Lithuania (2027), Romania (2027), Latvia (2028), Poland (2033).

De facto via welfare standards: Germany (last farm closed 2019); Sweden (fox farming ended 2005, chinchilla 2014, and mink in 2025 under a government buy-out, so no fur farms remain).

Sources: Fur Free Alliance, fur-ban tracker; Eurogroup for Animals (2025). Finland and Greece remain the two largest EU producers with no ban.

*Austria and Germany are the two standards closest to the EFSA recommendation of much more space and open-water access.

How the compliance costs are calculated

The cost of each standard is the one-time capital needed to rebuild the Finnish mink industry's cages to that standard, plus the yearly cost of financing that capital. It draws on four cost bases. The Finnish draft decree is the one standard costed directly, from the Natural Resources Institute Finland (Luke) construction-cost assessment. The larger Ecorys cages (from the European Commission's consultation survey) and the German statutory enclosure have no published construction-cost study, so they are priced with the same Luke per-metre rates extended to the extra cage area. The German figure is cross-checked against an independent 2014 expert valuation of a real German mink farm (TG-International). Each reported figure is the area-only floor: the construction and cage cost alone. The Finnish decree needs no assumption-costed features and is costed in full; the larger cages are reported as 'at least' that value, because features they also require but whose unit cost is only an assumption (a swimming basin, a solid floor, climbing structures) are excluded from the floor and push the true figure higher.

Finland: measured directly. The draft decree enlarges the cage floor from 2,550 to 3,600 cm² per adult. Luke prices this as shade-house running metres: converting the whole existing stock (137,500 m at about EUR 378 per metre in 2025 prices) and building 45,000 m of new capacity (at about EUR 540 per metre). This is spread over the Finnish mink sector's standard annual output of about 1,000,000 pelts: the normal full-capacity figure Luke uses as its denominator, for the whole national industry. That gives about EUR 76 of one-time capital per pelt. (Actual output has fallen far below capacity, to about 350,000 pelts in 2024, so on current output every per-pelt figure here would be roughly 2.9 times higher; the figures are kept on Luke's standard-output basis so they match the source.) This is the one directly-measured anchor; every other figure is calibrated so that the method below reproduces this Luke number exactly.

Ecorys: extrapolated from Luke. The Commission's survey (processed by Ecorys) sets larger cages under its reform scenarios: a two-level cage of 4,380 cm² per animal, or a single-level 5,400 cm². We price the extra floor area beyond the current cage at the same Luke rates, with the two-level cage's upper shelf added as hardware (see the table note). The area-only floor is at least EUR 150 per pelt of annual output for the two-level cage and at least EUR 218 for the single-level one. The two floors are closer than the extra cage area each needs (134,000 versus 485,000 m²) would suggest, because both carry the same shade-house conversion cost and the two-level cage's smaller footprint is partly offset by building its upper shelf. A partly-solid floor and enrichment (about EUR 9 to 12) are excluded from the floor. The enrichment is a basket of basic toys, priced from the only published mink cage-enrichment cost (Meagher et al., 2014), so it is a lower bound. There is no open water requirement.

German: anchored to an independent valuation. The German fur-farming statute requires at least 1 m² of floor per animal, roughly four times the current area, plus a solid floor, climbing structures and a swimming basin. The area-only construction floor is at least EUR 422 per pelt of annual output. The required swimming basin, solid floor and climbing structures are not included in this calculation, so the true figure is higher. As an independent check, the 2014 expert report valued a real German mink farm at about EUR 777 per pelt of annual output in 2025 prices, or about EUR 614 for the cages alone, well above the floor. The floor is a conservative lower bound, and the real-farm figures (over decade old but inflation adjusted) show the cost the German standard actually implies, which is why German is treated as the empirically-anchored high standard rather than a pure extrapolation.

Swimming pool cost: not measured. No published price exists for a farm swimming basin meeting the German requirement (1 m² of water, kept from freezing). The basin, the solid floor and the climbing structures that the German enclosure standard also requires are not included in the cost calculation. All three are therefore excluded from the reported floor and form part of its open-ended top. The German basin is a single 1 m² minimum per enclosure, with no cap on the number of mink kept in it, so its cost per mink depends on the build and falls as the enclosure is stocked more densely. In practice no German fur farm operates under this statute today. The Finnish and Ecorys standards require no water and carry no such cost.

Notes

  1. What the cost figures on each cage mean. 'Initial investment per mink' is a one-time outlay: the capital to rebuild the Finnish mink industry from the current legal cage to that standard. The three 'per pelt' lines below it are annual unit costs. 'Additional capital cost per pelt' is that one-time investment spread over the cage's 15-year life as an equal yearly charge (a capital-recovery annuity at a 3% real discount rate); 'Current production cost per pelt' is the yearly cost of producing a pelt (feed, labour and the rest, about EUR 39), unchanged by the standard; and 'Total cost per pelt with the new standards' is the two added together, that is, the break-even pelt price at that standard. All in 2025 EUR at Finnish construction prices, per pelt of standard annual output, and derived in the section above. The current legal cage is the cost baseline, so its additional capital is zero and its total is the production cost alone. Figures are rounded to whole euros, so the additional capital and production cost may differ from the total shown by about one euro.
  2. How row E's per-pelt areas are computed (the 0.8 and 1.3 coefficients). Each pelt of annual output carries three shares of cage space: the pelted juvenile's own place, a quarter of a female place, and a twentieth of a male place. The quarter comes from Luke's herd structure, where 250,000 breeding females produce the standard 1,000,000 pelts at four pelts per female per year, so each pelt carries a quarter of a female place. The twentieth is that quarter divided by five, on the assumption of one breeding male per five females (Luke's totals include males without giving a count). No time or day weighting is involved; the shares are herd composition. The juvenile term is where the standards differ. The Finnish rules state cage sizes per adult and house weaned juveniles in pairs, so a juvenile's allowance is half a cage, and the three shares (a half, a quarter and a twentieth) give a coefficient of 0.8: the current 2,550 cm² cage then gives 2,040 cm² (0.204 m²) per pelt, and the 3,600 cm² decree cage 0.288 m². The Ecorys survey and the German statute state their allowances per animal, each juvenile with its own unit, so the juvenile term is a full allowance and the shares give 1.3: the two-level 4,380 cm² cage then gives 5,694 cm² (0.57 m²) per pelt, the single-level 5,400 cm² gives 7,020 cm² (0.70 m²), and the German 1 m² per animal gives 1.30 m².
  3. Austria. 20 m² per adult pair (10 m² per mink: 5 m² floor plus 5 m² water), 50% of the area as water at ≥0.5 m depth, height 2.5 m; binding captive-wildlife ordinance. Like the German statute, this is an area-per-animal enclosure specification rather than a fixed cage (both drawn with a dashed outline); but here the water is half the area, so it scales with the number of animals, unlike Germany's fixed 1 m² basin. Fur farming is separately banned in Austria (2005), so no compliance cost is calculated; the enclosure is shown for scale.
  4. Germany, fur-farming statute. Floor ≥1 m² per animal (and ≥3 m² per enclosure), plus an additive swimming basin ≥1 m² at ≥0.3 m depth, height ≥1 m. The statute is an area-per-animal specification, not a fixed cage: the floor scales with the number of animals (1 m² each, above the 3 m² enclosure minimum), but the basin is a single per-enclosure minimum of 1 m² total, whatever the number of mink, so its per-animal share falls as the enclosure is stocked. Both the schematic and the bar chart read the statute per mink as 1 m² floor plus the full 1 m² basin: the floor is the per-animal minimum, and the basin is shown at its per-enclosure total.
  5. Ecorys 1 and 2 (EC survey, Scenarios 4 and 5). Two-level cage of 4,380 cm² per animal (base 90×30 cm plus an upper 56×30 cm shelf, 45 cm per level); or a single-level alternative of 5,400 cm² per animal. No open water.
  6. Finland, draft fur decree. 3,600 cm² per adult, at least 30 cm wide and 70 cm long (excluding the nest box), height ≥45 cm, no water (the explanatory memorandum declines swimming water, citing disease risk). At the 30 cm minimum width the cage is about 120 cm long to reach 3,600 cm², as shown.
  7. Sweden, mink cage law. Since 2013 Sweden has required mink to be kept in a two-level ('etage') cage: a lower level of at least 2,550 cm² (minimum 30 cm wide, 80 cm long, 45 cm high) plus an upper level of at least 900 cm² (30 × 30 cm), 3,450 cm² in total, with a nest box and a lying shelf that are not counted in the area (SJVFS 2019:16, 2 kap. 13 §). The lower level is drawn at 30 × 85 cm, the size needed to reach 2,550 cm² at the 30 cm minimum width. This standard is close to Finland's proposed 3,600 cm², but Swedish fur farming has since ended: fox farming ceased in 2005 and chinchilla in 2014 after welfare rules made them uneconomic, and the last mink farms were emptied by June 2025 under a government buy-out (there is no formal legislative ban yet). Shown as a comparator, not costed for Finland.
  8. EU current legal minimum / WelFur. 2,550 cm² per adult, at least 30 cm wide and 70 cm long (excluding the nest box), height 45 cm, no open water access. At the 30 cm minimum width the cage is about 85 cm long to reach 2,550 cm², as shown. This is the current standard and the cost baseline. WelFur's best score equals the legal cage and adds only scored, substitutable enrichment.
  9. Occupancy basis (bar chart). The fur-farm, Ecorys and German zoo-guidance figures are stated per animal. The AZA, Swiss and Austrian minima are stated per enclosure or per pair, and shown per mink at the standard's stated capacity of two animals, a conservative reading: a singly-housed mink would have up to about twice the land and water shown. The German fur statute is shown per mink as its 1 m² floor plus the full 1 m² basin (see Note 4); unlike the other rows, its basin does not scale per mink.
