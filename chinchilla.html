Chinchilla fur farming: the cost of raising the welfare standard

What it would cost to move chinchilla fur farms from the current cage to each proposed welfare standard, per pelt of annual output. Chinchilla is the one fur species that still makes money: a pelt price of about EUR 53 covers a production cost of about EUR 25. Raising the standard adds an order-of-magnitude capital cost, but under the modest reform cages costed here it does not by itself turn the activity loss-making; only much larger, EFSA-level enclosures would erase the margin.

Read every chinchilla figure as an order of magnitude

Chinchilla is not in the Finnish draft decree and is not farmed in Finland, so there is no Finnish construction-cost study to calibrate against and no Finland column. Its costs are built by analogy: the mink new-build cage rate (about EUR 342 per square metre) applied to the extra floor area above current practice. Three unsourced assumptions drive every number: current practice of about 0.10 m² per animal (the subtraction baseline); about 1.5 animals per pelt; and the mink build rate applied to a much smaller animal, which likely overstates a chinchilla cage's cost. The pelt price (about EUR 53) is an EU auction figure carried forward from 2022, and the production cost (about EUR 25) is the operating cost of a single Polish farm on 2011-2012 data, both via Carpenter (2025); neither is a Finnish or EU-wide chinchilla cost study. Treat the whole page as a rough order of magnitude, among the heaviest caveats of any species in this study.

“The majority of the welfare consequences (WCs) listed cannot be prevented or substantially mitigated within the current husbandry systems.”

“… substantial mitigation would require enclosures that provide sufficient three-dimensional space to incorporate structures and resources that enhance environmental complexity, to allow the quantitative and qualitative expression of fundamental behaviours (e.g. sustained locomotory activity, exploration, foraging and vigilance).”

For chinchillas, EFSA identifies six most relevant welfare consequences: restriction of movement, predation stress, the inability to perform exploratory or foraging behaviour, sensorial under- and overstimulation, the inability to perform comfort behaviour and resting problems. It finds that restriction of movement and the inability to perform exploratory or foraging behaviour cannot be prevented in the current cage, and reports no scientific information on how large an increase in floor area would substantially mitigate the restriction of movement. Providing a quartz-free sand bath can prevent the one comfort behaviour the cage can readily deliver, sand-bathing.

EFSA Panel on Animal Health and Welfare (2025). Welfare of American mink, red and Arctic foxes, raccoon dog and chinchilla kept for fur production (Scientific Opinion 9519). EFSA Journal, 23(7).

Chinchilla enclosure space: the fur cage against pet-keeping and captive standards

Minimum floor area allowed per chinchilla under each standard, on a single linear scale. Even pet-keeping law asks for more room than the fur cage: Switzerland and Austria require about 0.25 to 0.5 m² per animal for a pet chinchilla, against an assumed 0.10 m² on the farm. Keeping chinchillas as a wild species (Austria) requires about 4 m² per animal, roughly forty times the farm cage.

Green bars are floor area per animal; a hatched bar means the standard sets no enclosure-height figure. The asterisk marks standards that set substantially more space and structural complexity than the current cage, the direction EFSA points to (EFSA sets no numeric chinchilla enclosure size and certifies none of these as adequate). Captive and pet figures are per animal at the standard's stated pair or group capacity, a conservative reading. Chinchillas are legally kept as pets, so the Swiss and Austrian pet-keeping minima are genuine per-animal floors, not zoo figures. Austria's 4 m² is its "kept as a wild species" rule (8 m² per pair, indoor plus outdoor, 2 m height), an upper bound. The German zoo-husbandry guidance (BMEL, 2014) sets the same 0.5 m² floor as the German fur statute but with 1.5 m height and climbing structures. The German fur statute also requires a sand bath, a 50% solid floor and group housing that the bar cannot show. Most of these standards require a sand bath (the fur statute, the German zoo guidance and the Austrian and Swiss pet-keeping rules); Austria's wild-species enclosure requires climbing structures and gnawing wood instead, and the current fur cage is the pre-reform baseline. How the compliance costs are calculated

Chinchilla has no Finnish construction-cost study and is not in the Finnish decree, so unlike the other species there is no directly-measured anchor and no engineering-versus-proportional range. The cost of each standard is built by analogy: the one-time capital is the extra cage floor a standard requires, above current practice, priced at the mink all-in new-build rate of about EUR 342 per square metre, plus the yearly cost of financing that capital. Because a chinchilla enclosure is a lighter structure than a mink shade-house cage, using the mink rate likely overstates the cost, so the figures are a conservative (high) order of magnitude.

Ecorys: the EC survey cage. The Commission's survey (processed by Ecorys) sets a 65 × 40 cm cage (about 0.26 m²) with juveniles paired. Above the 0.15 m² per-pelt current-practice baseline (0.10 m² per animal over about 1.5 animals per pelt) this adds only a little floor, so the one-time capital is about EUR 43 per pelt and the break-even pelt price is about EUR 29, still well below the EUR 53 price.

German: the fur-farming statute. The German statute requires at least 0.5 m² of floor per adult (0.3 m² per weaned juvenile) and at least 1 m² per enclosure, a height of 1 m, a floor solid over at least half its area, a platform and a quartz-free sand bath per animal, and group housing. Pricing that extra floor and the solid-floor insert gives about EUR 153 per pelt of one-time capital and a break-even of about EUR 38, still below the price.

EFSA-level: illustrative only. EFSA sets no numeric chinchilla enclosure size, so there is no EFSA-level figure to cost. To show the order of magnitude, the table below carries three illustrative enclosures at two, three and five times the German floor area; these are not a reported EFSA-level standard. The margin narrows to roughly zero at about twice the German floor (break-even about EUR 53, level with the price) and turns clearly negative from about three times (break-even about EUR 69).

Chinchilla is the one profitable fur species: price against the reform break-evens

The chinchilla pelt price and current production cost against the break-even pelt price each standard would require. There is no price-history chart for chinchilla, because no annual auction price series is published for it. The single price (about EUR 53) sits above the current cost and above the Ecorys and German break-evens, and only the illustrative EFSA-level enclosures lift the break-even past it.

Each bar is the break-even pelt price a standard would require (production cost plus the annualised cost of the extra cage), coloured by how much space the standard adds: green for the most stringent (illustrative EFSA-level), amber for the German statute, red for the Ecorys cage, grey for the current baseline. The solid line is the current pelt price of about EUR 53; the dotted line is the current production cost of about EUR 25. Price and cost are Carpenter's (2025) figures, the cost on 2011-2012 Polish data and the price carried forward from a 2022 EU auction.

Notes

  1. What the cost figures are. One-time capital and the derived per-pelt cost of raising a chinchilla farm from the current cage to each standard, in 2025 EUR, per pelt of annual output. There is a single costing route (extra floor area above current practice, priced at the mink new-build rate), because chinchilla has no Finnish cage stock to convert. The break-even pelt price adds the production cost of about EUR 25 to the annualised capital; the current pelt price is about EUR 53.
  2. Chinchilla still makes money. Unlike mink, fox and raccoon dog, the chinchilla pelt price covers the production cost, so the activity is profitable before any reform. On Carpenter's (2025) account, EU chinchilla farming earned a positive aggregate profit of about EUR 3.8 million in 2024, the only one of the four species estimated as profitable, while mink, fox and raccoon dog ran at a loss. The reform standards add cost, but the Ecorys and German cages leave the price above break-even; only illustrative EFSA-level enclosures would erase the margin. Chinchilla is not farmed in Finland and is not covered by the Finnish draft decree.
  3. Three bare assumptions. Every chinchilla capital figure depends on three unsourced assumptions with no chinchilla-specific source: current practice of about 0.10 m² per animal (an error here scales every figure), about 1.5 animals per pelt, and the mink per-square-metre build rate applied to a smaller animal, which likely overstates the cost. The figures are an order of magnitude only, among the heaviest caveats of any species in this study.
  4. Ecorys (EC survey, Scenarios 4 and 5). A 65 × 40 cm cage (about 0.26 m²) at all production stages, with juveniles housed in pairs. The area is stated per cage with juveniles paired, so it is read here as about 0.13 m² per juvenile and 0.26 m² per adult, the same per-cage-versus-per-animal treatment as the mink page.
  5. Germany, fur-farming statute (TierErzHaVerbG). At least 0.5 m² of floor per adult and 0.3 m² per weaned juvenile, at least 1 m² per enclosure, height at least 1 m, a floor solid over at least half its area, at least one platform per animal, a quartz-free sand bath of at least 250 cm² per animal, additional boxes, and group housing. German zoo-husbandry guidance (BMEL, 2014) sets the same 0.5 m² floor for the true chinchilla but with 1.5 m height and climbing structures.
  6. EFSA-level is illustrative. EFSA sets no numeric chinchilla enclosure size, and reports no scientific information on how large an increase in floor area would substantially mitigate the restriction of movement. The EFSA-level rows in the tables are illustrative enclosures at two, three and five times the German floor area, shown only to give the order of magnitude; they are not a reported EFSA-level standard.
  7. Production cost and price. About EUR 25 production cost and about EUR 53 pelt price, both from Carpenter (2025), the cost from a single Polish farm's 2011-2012 data and the price carried forward from a 2022 EU auction, not uprated to 2025, so the margin is indicative rather than a current-year figure.
