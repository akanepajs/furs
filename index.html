Mink fur farming: enclosure space and the cost of raising the standard

A rough schematic of what a single mink is allowed under each fur-farming standard (green = floor area, blue = open-water pool), with the one-time and per-pelt cost of moving the Finnish industry to that standard. Every reform standard that has been costed lands well above the pelt price of about EUR 25: the current cages already run at a loss, and each step up in space adds cost, not revenue.

Fur farming already banned or being phased out across the EU*

Full national ban in force: Austria, Belgium, Bulgaria (mink), Croatia, Czechia, France, Ireland, Italy, Luxembourg, Malta, Netherlands, Slovakia, Slovenia.

Phase-out under way: Estonia (2026), Latvia (2028), Lithuania (2027), Poland (2033), Romania (2027).

De facto via welfare standards: Germany (last farm closed 2019).

Sources: Fur Free Alliance, fur-ban tracker; Eurogroup for Animals (2025). Finland and Greece remain the two largest EU producers with no ban.

*Direction consistent with the EFSA recommendation: much more space and open-water access.

How the compliance costs are calculated

The cost of each standard is the one-time capital needed to rebuild the Finnish mink industry's cages to that standard, plus the yearly cost of financing that capital. Four cost bases feed it. The Finnish draft decree is the one standard costed directly, from the Natural Resources Institute Finland (Luke) construction-cost assessment. The larger Ecorys cages (from the European Commission's consultation survey) and the German statutory cage have no published construction-cost study, so they are priced with the same Luke per-metre rates extended to the extra cage area. The German figure is cross-checked against an independent 2014 expert valuation of a real German mink farm (TG-International). The swimming basin that the German standard requires has no published unit cost and is carried as a flagged assumption.

Finland: measured directly. The draft decree enlarges the cage floor from 2,550 to 3,600 cm² per adult. Luke prices this as shade-house running metres: converting the whole existing stock (137,500 m at about EUR 378 per metre in 2025 prices) and building 45,000 m of new capacity (at about EUR 540 per metre). This is spread over the Finnish mink sector's standard annual output of about 1,000,000 pelts: the normal full-capacity figure Luke uses as its denominator, for the whole national industry, not a single model farm. That gives about EUR 76 of one-time capital per pelt. (Actual output has fallen far below capacity, to about 350,000 pelts in 2024, so on current output every per-pelt figure here would be roughly 2.9 times higher; the figures are kept on Luke's standard-output basis so they match the source.) This is the one directly-measured anchor; every other figure is calibrated so that the method below reproduces this Luke number exactly.

Ecorys: extrapolated from Luke. The Commission's survey (processed by Ecorys) sets larger cages under its reform scenarios: a two-level cage of 4,380 cm² per animal, or a single-level 5,400 cm². No one has costed these, so the extra floor area beyond the current cage is priced at the same Luke rates, with small added costs for a partly-solid floor and enrichment (and, for the two-level cage, its upper shelf as hardware, see the table note). The result is about EUR 164 to 345 per pelt for the two-level cage and EUR 235 to 469 for the single-level one, the range reflecting the two costing routes described below. There is no open water.

German: anchored to an independent valuation. The German fur-farming statute requires 1 m² of floor per animal, roughly four times the current area, plus a solid floor, climbing structures and a swimming basin. Modelling that cage on Finnish construction prices gives about EUR 552 to 1,124 per pelt. As an independent check, the 2014 expert report valued a real German mink farm at about EUR 777 per pelt of annual output in 2025 prices, or about EUR 614 for the cages alone. Both fall inside the modelled range, which is why German is treated as the empirically-anchored high standard rather than a pure extrapolation.

Swimming pool: an order-of-magnitude assumption. No published price exists for a farm swimming basin meeting the German requirement (at least 1 m² of water per animal, kept from freezing). It is included as an assumption of EUR 100 per pelt for the basin plus EUR 10 for climbing structures, not a measured cost. The German cage's solid floor adds about EUR 20 more (modelled from its area), so its basin, floor and climbing adders total about EUR 130. This is the least certain part of the German figure. The Finnish and Ecorys standards require no water and carry no such cost.

Under the Finnish standards weaned juveniles are pair-housed (counted as half a cage each), so one pelt maps to about 0.8 of an adult cage: the current 2,550 cm² cage becomes 0.204 m² per pelt and the 3,600 cm² decree cage 0.288 m². Under the Ecorys and German standards each juvenile has its own cage, so one pelt maps to about 1.3 cages (for example the German 1 m² per animal becomes 1.30 m² per pelt).

Notes

  1. What the cost figures are. One-time capital and the derived per-pelt cost of raising the Finnish mink industry from the current legal cage to each standard, in 2025 EUR at Finnish construction prices, per pelt of annual output (roughly per animal place). Ranges are two costing routes: the engineering route (convert existing shade-house stock, new-build the extra area at Luke's unit rates) at the low end, and the added-area-proportional route at the high end. "Total cost per pelt" adds the production cost of about EUR 39 (Carpenter 2025 reports EUR 36 for 2024) to the annualised capital; the current pelt price is about EUR 25 (2023 auction year). "Capital cost per pelt" is that capital annualised over 15 years at a 3% real discount rate. The current legal cage is the baseline: its capital is already sunk, so the increment over it is zero.
  2. Austria. 20 m² per adult pair (10 m² per mink: 5 m² floor plus 5 m² water), 50% of the area as water at ≥0.5 m depth, height 2.5 m; binding captive-wildlife ordinance. Fur farming is separately banned in Austria (2005), so no compliance cost is calculated; the enclosure is shown for scale.
  3. Germany, fur-farming statute. Floor ≥1 m² per animal (and ≥3 m² per enclosure), plus an additive swimming basin ≥1 m² at ≥0.3 m depth, height ≥1 m. The floor area shown here (1 m² per animal) is the per-animal minimum the cost model uses, drawn alongside the full 1 m² basin; the bar chart below instead reads the same statute at a two-animal enclosure occupancy (1.5 m² floor and 0.5 m² water per mink). The only binding EU open-water requirement for farmed mink.
  4. Ecorys 1 and 2 (EC survey, Scenarios 4 and 5). Two-level cage of 4,380 cm² per animal (base 90×30 cm plus an upper 56×30 cm shelf, 45 cm per level); or a single-level alternative of 5,400 cm² per animal. No open water.
  5. Finland, draft fur decree. 3,600 cm² per adult, height ≥45 cm, no water (the explanatory memorandum declines swimming water, citing disease risk).
  6. EU legal minimum / WelFur (current). 2,550 cm² per adult, minimum cage 30 cm wide × 70 cm long (excluding the nest box), height 45 cm, no water (Finnish decree 1084/2011, following the Council of Europe 1999 baseline). The current cage shares the draft decree's 30 × 70 cm minimum footprint and 45 cm height; the reform raises only the floor area, from 2,550 to 3,600 cm². This is the current standard and the cost baseline. WelFur's best score equals the legal cage and adds only scored, substitutable enrichment.
  7. Occupancy basis (bar chart). Fur-farm, Ecorys and German-guidance figures are stated per animal; the AZA, Swiss, Austrian and German-statute enclosure minima are stated per enclosure or per pair and shown per mink at the standard's stated capacity (two animals), a conservative reading. A singly-housed mink would have up to about twice the land and water shown for those rows.
  8. Bar length is true to area on a single linear scale, so the fur-farm and proposed cages appear as thin slivers: that gap is the finding, not a rendering artefact. Green shade encodes required height, blue shade water depth, from light gray (least) to medium colour (most); a hatched fill marks a dimension the source does not specify.
